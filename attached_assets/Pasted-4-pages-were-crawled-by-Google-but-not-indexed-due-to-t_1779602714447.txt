Heading: "How to Write Viral Tweet Threads"
Bullet points:
- Start with a hook — your first tweet must stop
  the scroll. Lead with a bold claim, surprising
  stat, or compelling question
- One idea per tweet — don't cram multiple points
  into one tweet. Each tweet should make one clear point
- Use numbered format — "1/" or "1/10" tells readers
  there's more coming and increases completion rate
- End with a call to action — ask readers to retweet,
  follow, or share their thoughts
- Keep sentences short — Twitter readers scan fast.
  Short punchy sentences perform better than long ones
- Add visuals — threads with images or videos get
  significantly more impressions

Heading: "Tweet Thread Best Practices"

hook → context → main points → conclusion → CTA.
Your opening tweet is the most important — if it
doesn't grab attention, nobody reads the rest.
Use numbers, strong opinions, or counterintuitive
ideas to make people stop scrolling.

Threads between 5-15 tweets perform best. Too short
and you don't provide enough value. Too long and
you lose readers before the end. Aim for the sweet
spot where every tweet adds value and builds on
the previous one."

- Never use irrelevant hashtags — hashtag stuffing
  with unrelated tags hurts credibility and reach

On Twitter/X, 1-3 targeted hashtags perform best —
the platform's algorithm deprioritizes posts that

LinkedIn works best with 3-5 professional hashtags.
Our formatter helps you prepare hashtag sets for

- Using too many hashtags on Twitter — reduces

Heading: "Understanding Daylight Saving Time"
Content:
"Daylight Saving Time (DST) shifts clocks forward
by one hour in spring and back in autumn in many
countries. This means the time difference between
two locations can change by an hour depending on
the time of year. The US, Canada, most of Europe,
and Australia observe DST — but India, Japan, China,
and most of Asia and Africa do not.

Our timezone converter automatically accounts for
DST so you always get the correct current time
difference without needing to manually track which
regions are currently observing DST."

Heading: "Tips for Scheduling Across Timezones"
Bullet points:
- Always confirm the timezone when scheduling —
  don't assume. Always specify the timezone when
  sharing meeting times internationally
- Use UTC as a reference — UTC never changes for
  DST, making it the most reliable reference point
  for international coordination
- Schedule in the overlap window — find the hours
  that fall within business hours for all participants
  to avoid early morning or late night calls
- Add world clock to your phone — keep 3-4 key
  timezones visible on your device if you work
  internationally
- Be aware of half-hour timezones — India (+5:30),
  Iran (+3:30), and a few others use 30-minute
  offsets that often surprise people

Heading: "Frequently Asked Questions"
Q: What is UTC?
A: UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is the primary
   time standard by which the world regulates clocks.
   It never observes daylight saving time, making it
   the most stable reference point for international
   scheduling.

Q: Does India observe daylight saving time?
A: No. India Standard Time (IST, UTC+5:30) does not
   observe daylight saving time and remains constant
   throughout the year.

Q: Why does the time difference between countries
   change throughout the year?
A: Because different countries start and end daylight
   saving time on different dates, the offset between
   them can shift by an hour during transition periods.
